Acid Reflux and GERD
Gastroesophageal reflux disease

Reflux now and then is normal. When it happens often or harms the food tube, it is called GERD, and about 1 in 5 U.S. adults has it. A ring of muscle, the LES valve (lower esophageal sphincter), sits where the food tube (the esophagus) meets the stomach. It opens to let food in, then shuts tight. In GERD it opens when it should not, or does not close well, so acid splashes up. The stomach has a coat that shields it from acid; the esophagus does not, so it burns (heartburn).

How it can feel
  • Burning behind the breastbone
  • A sour taste, or food coming up
  • Hoarse voice, throat clearing
  • A dry cough that hangs on
  • Upset stomach after meals
  • Bad breath, worn tooth enamel

Why treat it? Left alone, acid can inflame, narrow or scar the esophagus.

Get care right away if
  • Food sticks, or it hurts to swallow
  • You throw up blood or coffee-ground bits
  • Your stools are black or tarry
  • You lose weight without trying
  • Chest pain: call 911. It can be the heart.

About acid-blocking medicines

PPIs (like omeprazole) and H2 blockers (like famotidine) help many people heal. But acid also helps you absorb nutrients, and the FDA has warned about low magnesium and broken bones with long-term, high-dose PPI use.1 Never stop one on your own: ask your pharmacist for a plan.

Lifestyle Modifications

Close the kitchen early

Finish eating 3 hours before you lie down.

Raise the head

Lift the head of your bed 6 to 8 inches.

Smaller meals

Eat slowly and stop before full.

A healthy weight

Even a few pounds lighter eases pressure on the valve.

Know your triggers

Fried food, chocolate, mint, caffeine, alcohol, citrus, tomato, fizzy drinks.

Stop smoking

Smoking relaxes the valve and slows healing.
