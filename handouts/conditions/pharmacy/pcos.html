Polycystic Ovary Syndrome
PCOS

PCOS is a common hormone condition. The ovaries make more androgens (male-type hormones, such as testosterone, that women normally make in small amounts) than usual. That can upset periods, ovulation and fertility, and it shows up in skin, hair, weight and mood. Genes play a part, but in most women with PCOS insulin resistance helps drive it, which is why food, movement, sleep and stress change how PCOS behaves.

1

The pancreas makes insulin. Insulin moves sugar out of the blood and into your cells for fuel.

2

Cells stop listening to insulin. Sugar stays in the blood, so the pancreas pumps out even more.

3

Extra insulin reaches the ovaries. They make extra androgens. Eggs stop ripening and many small follicles (the “cysts”) are left behind.

PCOS facts

Up to 1 in 8 women of childbearing age has PCOS, and up to 7 in 10 of them have never been diagnosed. It happens at every body size, even at a healthy weight. Adults are diagnosed with 2 of 3: irregular periods, high androgens (a blood test or signs like extra hair) or many follicles on ultrasound. You can have PCOS without “cysts.”

Risk factors

  • Runs in families: a mother or sister with PCOS or type 2 diabetes raises your risk.
  • You can change: a high-sugar, low-nutrient diet, sitting most of the day, weight gain at the waist, poor sleep and chronic stress, hormone-disrupting chemicals.
  • Why it matters: PCOS raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and sleep apnea, so ask your pharmacist about checking your blood sugar.

Lifestyle Recommendations

Diet

Protein and fiber at every meal. Limit refined sugar and starch to steady blood sugar.

Exercise

150 minutes a week of activity plus 2 strength days improves insulin sensitivity.

Sleep

Seven to eight hours a night supports weight and healthy hormone production.

Limit hormone disruptors

Glass or steel containers, never heat food in plastic, fragrance-free products.
