Polycystic Ovary Syndrome
PCOS, now also called PMOS

PCOS is a common hormone condition. The ovaries make extra androgens. These are male-type hormones like testosterone; women normally make a little. Too much can upset periods, ovulation (releasing an egg) and fertility, and it shows up in skin, hair, weight and mood. Genes play a part, but in most women with PCOS insulin resistance (cells answer insulin poorly) helps drive it. That is why food, movement, sleep and stress change how PCOS behaves.

1

The pancreas makes insulin. Insulin moves sugar out of the blood and into your cells for fuel.

2

Cells answer insulin poorly. So the pancreas pumps out extra insulin to keep sugar in check.

3

Extra insulin reaches the ovaries. The ovaries make extra androgens, and eggs get stuck in follicles (the “cysts”).

PCOS facts

About 1 in 8 women of childbearing age has PCOS, and up to 7 in 10 of them have never been diagnosed. It happens at every body size, even at a healthy weight. Adults are diagnosed with 2 of 3: irregular periods, high androgens (a blood test or signs like extra hair) or many follicles on ultrasound (or a blood test showing high AMH, a follicle hormone), once other causes are ruled out. You can have PCOS without “cysts.” In 2026 experts renamed it polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), because the “cysts” are really stalled egg follicles. Same condition, same tests.

Risk factors

  • Runs in families: a mother or sister with PCOS or type 2 diabetes raises your risk.
  • Diabetes: type 1 or 2 raises the risk.
  • Can make it worse (you can change): weight gain at the waist, a high-sugar diet, sitting most of the day, poor sleep, stress and hormone-disrupting chemicals (in some plastics and fragrances).

Why it matters: PCOS raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and sleep apnea (breathing pauses in sleep), so ask your doctor about a blood sugar test. Long gaps between periods can thicken the womb lining, so tell your doctor if yours are often months apart.

Lifestyle Recommendations

Protein first

Start every meal with protein. Never eat carbs on their own, and skip sugary drinks.

Feed your gut

Plain kefir, yogurt or sauerkraut daily, plus 4 to 6 servings of vegetables.

Move and lift

At least 30 minutes on most days, plus strength work 2 days a week.

Sleep 7 to 9 hours

Snoring or always tired? Ask about sleep apnea.

Spearmint tea

Two cups a day. In a small 30-day study, testosterone came down.

Pharmacist checkKeep taking your medicines.

  • Trying to get pregnant, pregnant or nursing? Do not take Berberine Pro. Myo-Inositol Complex has fenugreek, which is not advised in pregnancy: ask your pharmacist before taking it or the rest.
  • Keep any prescribed medicine. On insulin or a sulfonylurea (such as glipizide)? Ask the prescriber before you start Berberine Pro or Myo-Inositol Complex, and watch for low blood sugar. Berberine can also slow the liver enzymes (CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9) that clear many drugs: ask your pharmacist to check your list.
  • On a blood thinner? The label says D3 5000 with K2 is not recommended: its vitamin K (K1 and K2) works against warfarin. Fish oil and fenugreek can also add to bleeding risk. Ask your pharmacist about a vitamin D without K.
  • Allergic to fish, peanuts or chickpeas? Omega Pure is fish oil, and the fenugreek in Myo-Inositol Complex, a legume like peanuts, can trigger it too: ask first.
  • The pill can lower B6, B12, folate, magnesium and zinc, and metformin can lower B12 over time: ask about a check.
