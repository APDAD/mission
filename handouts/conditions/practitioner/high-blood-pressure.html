High Blood Pressure
No symptoms, real risk

Nearly half of U.S. adults have high blood pressure, and about 4 in 10 of them do not know it, because it rarely causes symptoms. Over the years, the extra force strains the heart, kidneys, brain and eyes and raises the risk of heart attack and stroke. One signal you can influence is nitric oxide (NO), which tells blood-vessel walls to relax. What you eat, how you move, how you sleep and how you handle stress all change how much of it your body makes.

Reading your numbers
118
Systolic (top number): the push on artery walls when your heart beats.
76
Diastolic (bottom number): the push between beats, while your heart rests.
Measure it right
  • No caffeine, smoking or exercise for 30 minutes. Rest 5 minutes first.
  • Sit back supported, feet flat, legs uncrossed, arm at heart level.
  • Cuff on bare skin. Take 2 readings, 1 minute apart. Write them down.
  • Ask your provider to check your cuff size.

Over 180/120? Rest a few minutes and check again. Still that high: call your doctor right away. With chest or back pain, shortness of breath, numbness, weakness, or trouble seeing or speaking, call 911.
Some medicines raise blood pressure, like decongestants in cold remedies and pain relievers such as ibuprofen and naproxen. Ask your provider first.

Lifestyle Recommendations

Eat for nitric oxide

Beets, arugula, spinach and celery bring nitrate. Load up on potassium-rich produce.

Cut the sodium

Aim under 1,500 to 2,300 mg a day. Most hides in packaged and restaurant food.

Move, then hold

Walk daily and add isometric holds (wall sits, hand grips) 3 times a week.

Sleep and breathe

Sleep 7 to 9 hours. Loud snoring? Ask about sleep apnea.

Quit smoking

Each cigarette tightens vessels. Keep alcohol low, too.

Skip harsh mouthwash

Antiseptic rinses wipe out the mouth bacteria that help make NO.
